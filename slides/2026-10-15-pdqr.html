Computing probabilities in R

Author
Affiliation

Dwija Kakkad

Where we are

Today

We’ve already practiced using R as a calculator, working with vectors, and evaluating probability mass functions.

Now we’ve met the cumulative distribution function (CDF) in lecture. Today we’ll use R to compute probabilities using the CDF.

Our priorities:

  • Recognize the probability model and its parameters;
  • Translate events into expressions involving the CDF;
  • Ask R to evaluate those expressions.
Note

The review slides are here for reference. We’ll move quickly through some of them.

Part I: Discrete random variables

Review: discrete random variables

A random variable \(X\) is discrete if its possible values form a finite or countably infinite set.

Its probability mass function (PMF) is

\[p_X(k)=P(X=k).\]

The PMF must satisfy

\[p_X(k)\geq 0,\qquad \sum_k p_X(k)=1.\]

To find the probability of an event, add the masses at the values in that event:

\[P(X\in A)=\sum_{k\in A}p_X(k).\]

Pictures of two discrete PMFs

A PMF assigns probability mass to individual values.

Neither picture is a density curve. The probability at an integer is the height of its spike.

Three families we’ve met

Family Interpretation Range Parameters
Binomial Number of successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials \(0,1,\ldots,n\) \(n, p\)
Poisson Count of events in a specified interval \(0,1,2,\ldots\) \(\lambda>0\)
Geometric Number of trials until the first success \(1,2,3,\ldots\) \(p\)

Different probability models describe different experiments. Identifying the model comes before choosing an R command.

Review: binomial

If \(X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)\), then

\[P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}, \quad k=0,\ldots,n.\]

Think: \(n\) independent trials, each with success probability \(p\); \(X\) counts successes.

For example, if \(X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(20,0.3)\):

# Probability of exactly 5 successes
dbinom(5, size = 20, prob = 0.3)
[1] 0.1788631

Review: Poisson

If \(X\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)\), then

\[P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}, \quad k=0,1,2,\ldots.\]

\(X\) often counts arrivals, occurrences, or defects; \(\lambda\) is the expected count for the interval being studied.

# Probability of exactly 5 events when lambda = 12
dpois(5, lambda = 12)
[1] 0.01274064

If the rate is 6 events/hour, the Poisson mean for a two-hour interval is 12, not 6 (assuming the usual Poisson-process model).

Review: geometric

Under our convention, \(X\) counts the trial number of the first success in independent Bernoulli trials with success probability \(p\):

\[P(X=k)=(1-p)^{k-1}p,\qquad k=1,2,3,\ldots.\]

We won’t use the geometric distribution in today’s R exercises. Our computational examples will use binomial and Poisson distributions.

The CDF

Definition

For any random variable \(X\), its cumulative distribution function (CDF) is

\[\boxed{F_X(x)=P(X\leq x).}\]

For discrete \(X\):

\[F_X(x)=\sum_{k\leq x}P(X=k).\]

For example, if \(X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(20,0.3)\):

\[P(X\leq 5)=\sum_{k=0}^{5}P(X=k).\]

. . .

sum(dbinom(0:5, size = 20, prob = 0.3))
[1] 0.4163708

From masses to a CDF

The CDF accumulates the probability mass as the threshold increases.

The CDF jumps at possible outcomes; the size of each jump is the corresponding PMF value.

Review: properties of every CDF

  • \(0\leq F_X(x)\leq 1\);
  • \(F_X(x)\) is nondecreasing;
  • \(F_X(x)\to 0\) as \(x\to-\infty\), and \(F_X(x)\to1\) as \(x\to\infty\);
  • \(F_X\) is right-continuous;
  • \(P(a<X\leq b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a)\), for \(a<b\).

For discrete \(X\), the CDF has jumps. The jump at \(x\) has size \(P(X=x)\).

The p functions in R

R uses a common naming scheme for probability distributions:

Family PMF or PDF CDF Simulation
Binomial dbinom() pbinom() rbinom()
Poisson dpois() ppois() rpois()
Uniform dunif() punif() runif()
Normal dnorm() pnorm() rnorm()

The p functions return \(P(X\leq x)\) (the lower tail) by default.

First calculation: a shortcut for summing masses

Let \(X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(20,0.3)\). Compute \(P(X\leq5)\) using the PMF and then the CDF.

# Long way: add the individual masses
sum(dbinom(0:5, size = 20, prob = 0.3))
[1] 0.4163708
# Short way: ask for the CDF
pbinom(5, size = 20, prob = 0.3)
[1] 0.4163708

They give the same answer. pbinom(5, ...) evaluates the cumulative probability directly.

See the complement

For \(X\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(12)\), the event \(X>10\) is the unshaded remainder when \(X\leq10\) is shaded.

\[P(X>10)=1-F_X(10).\]

One formula, many probabilities

For any \(a<b\),

\[P(a<X\leq b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a).\]

And by the complement rule,

\[P(X>a)=1-F_X(a).\]

Warning: For a discrete random variable, \(P(X< a)\) and \(P(X\leq a)\) need not be equal.

Part II: Translating probability statements

Your turn: Poisson probabilities

Let \(X\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(12)\). Write an R expression for each probability. Don’t execute anything until you’ve decided which endpoints to use.

  1. \(P(X\leq10)\)
  2. \(P(X>10)\)
  3. \(P(X<10)\)
  4. \(P(X\geq10)\)
  5. \(P(8\leq X\leq15)\)
  6. \(P(8<X<15)\)

Allow time to discuss the endpoint adjustments, especially the differences between 2/4 and 5/6.

Solutions: Poisson probabilities

ppois(10, lambda = 12)               # P(X <= 10)
[1] 0.3472294
1 - ppois(10, lambda = 12)           # P(X > 10)
[1] 0.6527706
ppois(9, lambda = 12)                # P(X < 10)
[1] 0.2423922
1 - ppois(9, lambda = 12)            # P(X >= 10)
[1] 0.7576078
ppois(15, 12) - ppois(7, 12)         # P(8 <= X <= 15)
[1] 0.7549112
ppois(14, 12) - ppois(8, 12)         # P(8 < X < 15)
[1] 0.6169968

Why 9 instead of 10? Why 7 instead of 8? We can move a strict inequality by one because a Poisson variable takes integer values.

Visualize the endpoints

For integer-valued \(X\), the two events below are not the same.

The only difference is the mass at \(X=10\).

Another way to compute upper tails

The function argument lower.tail = FALSE asks R for \(P(X>x)\).

# Two ways to compute P(X > 10)
1 - ppois(10, lambda = 12)
[1] 0.6527706
ppois(10, lambda = 12, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 0.6527706

These are mathematically equivalent. The second is often preferable for very small tail probabilities because it avoids subtracting a number extremely close to 1.

It still means \(P(X>10)\), not \(P(X\geq10)\).

Your turn: a binomial variable

Let \(Y\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(30,0.2)\).

Write R expressions for:

  1. \(P(Y=4)\);
  2. \(P(Y\leq4)\);
  3. \(P(Y\geq4)\);
  4. \(P(3\leq Y<8)\).

Try to use pbinom() whenever it is appropriate, and dbinom() for an individual mass.

Solutions: binomial probabilities

dbinom(4, size = 30, prob = 0.2)
[1] 0.1325224
pbinom(4, size = 30, prob = 0.2)
[1] 0.2552333
pbinom(3, size = 30, prob = 0.2, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 0.8772892
pbinom(7, 30, 0.2) - pbinom(2, 30, 0.2)
[1] 0.7166116

Sanity check: Every result must lie between 0 and 1. The probability of \(Y\geq4\) should exceed the probability of \(Y=4\).

Part III: Continuous random variables

Review: continuous random variables

A continuous random variable is described by a probability density function (PDF) \(f_X\) such that

\[f_X(x)\geq0,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(x)\,dx=1.\]

Probabilities are areas under the density curve:

\[P(a<X<b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx.\]

Crucially, \(f_X(x)\) is a density, not \(P(X=x)\).

For a continuous random variable with a PDF,

\[\boxed{P(X=x)=0\quad\text{for every }x.}\]

What does a PDF represent?

A probability is an area, not the height of a density curve.

\[P(-1<Z<1)=F_Z(1)-F_Z(-1).\]

The continuous CDF

The definition has not changed:

\[F_X(x)=P(X\leq x).\]

For a continuous random variable with density \(f_X\),

\[F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(t)\,dt.\]

Therefore,

\[P(a<X<b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a).\]

Because individual points have zero probability, the strictness of the endpoints doesn’t matter for intervals:

\[P(a<X<b)=P(a\leq X\leq b).\]

Discrete versus continuous

Probability statement Integer-valued \(X\) Continuous \(Y\)
\(P( X < 10)\) or \(P(Y<10)\) \(F_X(9)\) \(F_Y(10)\)
\(P(X\leq10)\) or \(P(Y\leq10)\) \(F_X(10)\) \(F_Y(10)\)
\(P(X=10)\) or \(P(Y=10)\) May be positive \(0\)
\(P(8<X<12)\) or \(P(8<Y<12)\) \(F_X(11)-F_X(8)\) \(F_Y(12)-F_Y(8)\)

The definition of the CDF is the same. The endpoint bookkeeping is different.

The continuous uniform distribution

\(X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(a,b)\) means that the density is constant between \(a\) and \(b\):

\[f_X(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{b-a},&a\leq x\leq b,\\[4pt]0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\]

For \(a\leq x\leq b\), its CDF is

\[F_X(x)=\frac{x-a}{b-a}.\]

For example, if \(X\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(2,8)\):

punif(5, min = 2, max = 8)  # P(X <= 5)
[1] 0.5

Uniform: density and CDF

The shaded area on the left equals the CDF’s height at \(x=5\) on the right.

The normal distribution

\(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) means \(X\) has a normal distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\).

Its density is

\[f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\},\quad \sigma>0.\]

The normal density is symmetric about \(\mu\). Its CDF cannot be expressed in elementary functions, but R can evaluate it numerically.

# N(100, 2^2): mean = 100, STANDARD DEVIATION = 2
pnorm(103, mean = 100, sd = 2)
[1] 0.9331928

R takes sd, not variance.

Normal: density and CDF

The vertical difference between CDF values at 97 and 103 equals the shaded PDF area.

A first continuous calculation

Suppose \(Y\sim N(100,2^2)\). Find \(P(97<Y<103)\).

\[P(97<Y<103)=F_Y(103)-F_Y(97).\]

. . .

pnorm(103, mean = 100, sd = 2) -
  pnorm(97, mean = 100, sd = 2)
[1] 0.8663856

Notice that for this continuous variable, we need no plus or minus 1 at the endpoints.

Don’t confuse d and p

For the normal family:

dnorm(100, mean = 100, sd = 2) # Height of the density
[1] 0.1994711
pnorm(100, mean = 100, sd = 2) # Area to the left
[1] 0.5

The first number is not \(P(Y=100)\); that probability is zero. The second is \(P(Y\leq100)=1/2\) by symmetry.

Part IV: Translating probability statements — your turn

Exercise 1: uniform

Let \(U\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(2,8)\).

Using punif() and ordinary arithmetic, calculate:

  1. \(P(U\leq5)\);
  2. \(P(U>6)\);
  3. \(P(3<U<7)\);
  4. \(P(U=5)\).

Then check answers 1–3 by calculating the corresponding lengths as fractions of the total interval length.

Practice: visualize before calculating

For \(Z\sim N(100,2^2)\), sketch the event \(|Z-100|>3\) before entering any code.

Exercise 2: normal

Let \(Z\sim N(100,2^2)\).

Using pnorm(), calculate:

  1. \(P(Z<97)\);
  2. \(P(Z\geq103)\);
  3. \(P(97<Z<103)\);
  4. \(P(|Z-100|>3)\).

Explain in words what event 4 describes. Which answers can be related by symmetry?

Exercise 3: choose your model

A factory makes components independently. Each component has probability \(0.04\) of being defective. An inspector tests 75 components.

  1. Specify a distribution for the number of defective components, including its parameters.
  2. Find the probability that at least five are defective.
  3. Find the probability that between two and six, inclusive, are defective.

Write the probability statement in mathematical notation before writing the R code.

Exercise 4: choose your model

Customers arrive at a shop according to a Poisson process at an average rate of 6 per hour.

  1. Specify a distribution for the number arriving in the next two hours.
  2. Find the probability of more than 15 arrivals.
  3. Find the probability of between 10 and 15 arrivals, inclusive.
  4. What would you change if the observation interval were 30 minutes?

Exercise 5: a manufacturing tolerance

The length \(L\) of a manufactured rod is normally distributed, with mean 100 mm and standard deviation 2 mm. A rod is accepted if its length is between 97 and 103 mm, inclusive.

  1. Calculate the proportion of rods that will be accepted.
  2. Calculate the proportion that will be rejected.
  3. If the acceptance interval were instead 98 to 102 mm, what would happen to the acceptance probability? Calculate it.

Exercise 6: conditional probability (challenge)

Let \(X\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(12)\).

A researcher learns that at least 10 events occurred but doesn’t know the exact count. Find

\[P(X\geq15\mid X\geq10).\]

Write this as a ratio of two unconditional probabilities before evaluating it in R.

Before you leave

You should be able to:

  • Identify the appropriate model and its parameters;
  • Translate \(<,\leq,>,\geq\) and interval events into CDF expressions;
  • Handle endpoints correctly for discrete versus continuous variables;
  • Use pbinom(), ppois(), punif(), and pnorm() to evaluate the results;
  • Recognize when d...() supplies a mass versus a density.

Exercise 5 solutions

accepted <- pnorm(103, 100, 2) - pnorm(97, 100, 2)
accepted
[1] 0.8663856
1 - accepted
[1] 0.1336144

[1] 0.6826895

Exercise 6 solution

Because \(\{X\geq15\}\subseteq\{X\geq10\}\),

\[P(X\geq15\mid X\geq10)=\frac{P(X\geq15)}{P(X\geq10)}.\]

ppois(14, 12, lower.tail = FALSE) /
  ppois(9, 12, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 0.3009149
